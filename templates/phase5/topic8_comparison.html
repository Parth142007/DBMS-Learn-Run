8.1

The Normalization Hierarchy & Nesting

Normal forms exist in a strict nesting hierarchy where each successive normal form adds a tighter constraint to the previous form:

Level 1
1NF

Atomic values only; no repeating groups

Level 2
2NF

1NF + No partial dependencies on composite keys

Level 3
3NF

2NF + No transitive dependencies

Level 3.5
BCNF

3NF + Every determinant is a superkey

Relation Set Inclusion: All Relations ³ 1NF ³ 2NF ³ 3NF ³ BCNF

Comprehensive Comparison Matrix

1NF
First Normal Form 		All attribute values must be atomic (indivisible). No repeating groups, arrays, or comma-separated lists. Multivalued / Non-atomic attributes Courses = "DBMS, Python" in a single column row. Create a row for each course value or split out a separate bridge entity with foreign keys.
2NF
Second Normal Form 		Must be in 1NF and have no partial functional dependencies. Non-prime attributes must depend on the whole candidate key. Partial Functional Dependencies:
$X \to A$ where $X \subset \text{Candidate Key}$ 		(StudentID, CourseID) → StudentName where StudentID → StudentName holds. Extract the determinant and dependent attribute into their own entity: STUDENT(StudentID, StudentName).
3NF
Third Normal Form 		Must be in 2NF and have no transitive dependencies. For every $X \to A$, either $X$ is superkey or $A$ is prime. Transitive Dependencies:
$Key \to Y \to A$ (non-key determines non-key) 		StudentID → DeptID and DeptID → DeptName in same relation. Extract the transitive chain: DEPARTMENT(DeptID, DeptName), keeping DeptID as FK in STUDENT.
BCNF
Boyce-Codd 		For every non-trivial $X \to A$, $X$ must be a superkey. (No prime-attribute exception). Dependencies where determinant is not a superkey (even if $A$ is prime) R(Student, Course, Instructor) where Instructor → Course and keys are {S, C} and {S, I}. Decompose on the non-superkey determinant: R1(Instructor, Course) and R2(Student, Instructor).
8.3

Real-World Engineering: Trade-offs & Denormalization

Is the highest normal form always the best engineering choice? No! Database design is an engineering discipline that balances theoretical purity against operational performance.

Benefits of High Normalization (3NF / BCNF)
  • Zero redundancy: Facts are stored in exactly one place.
  • Guaranteed consistency: Update, insertion, and deletion anomalies are virtually eliminated.
  • Minimal storage overhead: Avoids duplicating repetitive strings across millions of rows.
  • Optimal for OLTP: Fast writes, updates, and transactions in high-throughput transactional applications.
Costs & Drawbacks of Excessive Splitting
  • Complex multi-table JOINs: Fetching a simple user profile might require joining 6 or 7 separate normalized tables.
  • Slower analytical queries: High computational cost for reports and aggregations.
  • Potential loss of dependency preservation: Decomposing to BCNF can make constraint enforcement slower.
  • Developer friction: More foreign keys, triggers, and query joins to maintain.
Standard Industry Practice: Most production relational databases are designed to 3NF for transactional systems (OLTP). In data warehouses and analytical systems (OLAP), intentional denormalization (such as Star or Snowflake schemas) is practiced to optimize read throughput.

Practice Exercises with Guided Solutions

Exercise 1 Normal Form Identification

Relation $R(A, B, C)$ has Candidate Key $A$. Dependencies: $A \to B, C$ and $B \to C$.
Question: What is the highest normal form satisfied by $R$?

Hint: Candidate key is single-attribute $\{A\}$. Can there be any partial dependency? Is there a transitive dependency?
Solution:
1. Candidate key is single attribute $A$, so partial dependencies are impossible → Satisfies 2NF.
2. In $B \to C$, $B$ is a non-prime attribute determining another non-prime attribute $C$ → Transitive dependency exists ($A \to B \to C$)!
3. Since $B$ is not a superkey and $C$ is not prime, it violates 3NF.
Conclusion: Highest normal form is 2NF.
Exercise 2 3NF vs. BCNF Distinction

State the condition under which a relation in 3NF is guaranteed to also be in BCNF without any further decomposition.

Hint: Think about candidate keys: what if there is only one candidate key, or no composite keys overlap?
Solution:
A relation in 3NF is guaranteed to be in BCNF if:
1. It has only a single candidate key, OR
2. All candidate keys are disjoint (non-overlapping) and single attributes.
BCNF can only differ from 3NF when there are overlapping composite candidate keys.
