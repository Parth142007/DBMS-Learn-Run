Lesson 7

Extended Entity-Relationship (EER) Concepts

Advanced Syllabus Topic
1. Specialization & Generalization

As real-world enterprise databases grow in complexity, classical ER modeling lacks constructs for object-oriented abstractions. The Extended ER (EER) Model introduces mechanisms to model taxonomies and hierarchies:

Top-Down Process
Specialization

The process of defining a set of subclasses from an existing higher-level entity type (the superclass). It distinguishes subsets of entities that possess unique, specialized attributes or relationships.

College Example: We start with PERSON(PersonID, Name, Email, DOB). Looking closer, we recognize that students have a GPA while instructors have a Salary and OfficeRoom. We specialize PERSON into subclasses STUDENT and INSTRUCTOR.
Bottom-Up Process
Generalization

The reverse design process: synthesizing multiple entity sets that share common properties into a single generalized superclass.

College Example: An analyst notices two separate entities: CAR(VehicleID, Price, MaxSeating) and TRUCK(VehicleID, Price, MaxPayload). By extracting common attributes VehicleID and Price, they generalize them into a higher-level superclass VEHICLE.
2. Superclass, Subclass & Attribute Inheritance

An entity in a subclass represents the exact same real-world object as an entity in the superclass, playing a specialized role. This establishes an IS-A relationship:

EER Hierarchy: A Student inherits PersonID, Name, and Email from PERSON, while maintaining its own distinct attribute GPA.
Type Inheritance Rule:
An entity that is a member of a subclass inherits all attributes of the superclass. Furthermore, it inherits all relationship participations of the superclass (e.g. if Person has an address, Student automatically has an address).
3. Constraints on Specialization / Generalization

EER hierarchies are governed by two orthogonal structural constraints: Disjointness and Completeness.

1. Disjointness Constraint (Disjoint vs Overlapping)

Specifies whether an entity can be a member of more than one subclass simultaneously:

  • Disjoint (Symbol: 'd'): An entity can be a member of at most one subclass. E.g., an EMPLOYEE is either Hourly OR Salaried, never both.
  • Overlapping (Symbol: 'o'): The same entity may simultaneously belong to multiple subclasses. E.g., in a university, a PERSON can be both a Student AND a teaching assistant (Employee).
2. Completeness Constraint (Total vs Partial)

Specifies whether every superclass entity MUST belong to at least one subclass:

  • Total Specialization (Double Line): Every entity in the superclass must belong to at least one subclass. E.g., every ACCOUNT in a bank must be either Savings or Checking.
  • Partial Specialization (Single Line): Some entities in the superclass might not belong to any subclass. E.g., a VEHICLE might be a Motorcycle, which is neither a Car nor a Truck.
EER Advanced Construct
Aggregation

In classical ER diagrams, relationships can only connect entities, never relationships directly. Aggregation is an abstraction through which relationships are treated as higher-level entities.

Example: Suppose an INSTRUCTOR Interviews a STUDENT. We want to record an EVALUATION of that specific interview. Aggregation encloses the relationship (INSTRUCTOR - Interviews - STUDENT) inside a higher-level box that connects to the EVALUATION relationship.
