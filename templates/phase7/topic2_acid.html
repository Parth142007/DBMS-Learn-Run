Lesson 2

The ACID Properties of Database Transactions

NEPC42 Core Concept
1. The Foundation of Reliable Transaction Processing

To guarantee that database data remains accurate, trustworthy, and consistent across software errors, hardware failures, power crashes, and concurrent multi-user access, database systems enforce the four ACID Properties. Coined originally by Jim Gray and Bruce Lindsay, ACID stands for Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, and Durability.

Critical Concept Clarification: Does ACID alone guarantee that application business rules are never broken? No! The database engine enforces technical primitives (atomicity of writes, isolation locks, redo logs), but correct business rules require database schema constraints (e.g. CHECK (balance >= 0), FOREIGN KEY, NOT NULL) combined with correct application logic.
2. The Four Pillars: Definition, Analogy, & Failure Modes
A — Atomicity ("All-or-Nothing")
Indivisible Unit

Definition: Either all operations within the transaction execute successfully to completion, or none of them have any lasting effect on the database. If an unexpected failure occurs at any point, the entire transaction is rolled back.

Real-World Analogy: Purchasing a flight ticket with an airport shuttle connection. If the airline confirms the flight but the shuttle is fully booked, the entire booking package is cancelled rather than leaving you stranded.
Bank DB Example: Transferring ₹1,000 from Account A to Account B requires UPDATE A (deduct 1000) and UPDATE B (add 1000). Atomicity ensures both updates succeed together.
If Atomicity Fails: Account A is debited ₹1,000, but a crash occurs before Account B is credited. The ₹1,000 vanishes from circulation, corrupting the bank's general ledger.
C — Consistency ("Preserving Invariants")
Integrity Preservation

Definition: A transaction takes the database from one valid state (satisfying all defined integrity constraints, foreign keys, uniqueness rules, and business assertions) to another valid state.

Real-World Analogy: Double-entry bookkeeping in accounting. Every credit in one account must be offset by an equal debit in another account, ensuring the balance sheet always zeroes out.
Bank DB Example: System invariant: Balance(A) + Balance(B) == ₹8,000 and Balance >= 0. After ₹1,000 transfer, sum is ₹4,000 + ₹4,000 = ₹8,000.
If Consistency Fails: Account A transfers ₹10,000 when its balance is only ₹5,000. Without a CHECK (balance >= 0) constraint, Account A goes into an illegal negative state.
I — Isolation ("Independence of Execution")
Concurrency Boundary

Definition: Intermediate data modifications made by one active transaction are invisible to other concurrently executing transactions until the transaction commits (governed by the active isolation level). Concurrent execution yields results equivalent to some serial execution.

Real-World Analogy: Individual voting booths in an election. Each voter casts their vote privately without observing or being influenced by partial ballots filled out by others simultaneously.
Bank DB Example: Transaction T1 is transferring ₹1,000 from A to B. Concurrently, an audit transaction T2 calculates total bank balances. T2 must not see A debited while B is not yet credited.
If Isolation Fails: A dirty read occurs: T2 reads uncommitted, temporary data from T1. T1 then rolls back, but T2 has already acted upon phantom numbers.
D — Durability ("Permanence After Commit")
Crash Survival

Definition: Once a transaction commits, its modifications are permanently recorded in non-volatile storage and will never be lost, even if an immediate hardware crash, power outage, or OS kernel panic occurs.

Real-World Analogy: Signing an official deed in permanent ink with a government stamp. Once registered, a subsequent power cut at the registrar's office does not void your legal ownership.
Bank DB Example: A customer deposits ₹5,000 and receives a confirmation receipt (COMMIT). Even if the server restarts 10 milliseconds later, the balance remains ₹5,000 via MySQL's Redo Log.
If Durability Fails: The transaction reports "Payment Successful" to the merchant, but because changes were only in volatile RAM without WAL flush, a server restart erases the payment record.

Scenario: Atomicity under Abrupt Failure
